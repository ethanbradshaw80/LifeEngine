# The Life Simulator — the Business update

*Everything since the last itch.io build (v163). For the devlog / update notes.*

---

## Run a business that actually runs

A business used to be a number that went up while you watched it. Now it is a
trade you keep, staff you hire, stock you order and a month that can go wrong.

- **Twenty trades**, each unlocking in its own era. A filling station and a
  feed store in 1970; a computer shop from the eighties; e-commerce from the
  mid-nineties; subscription software after 2002. Some of them **end** — a
  video rental shop is a fine business in 1985 and a tragedy by 2010, and the
  game will let you live that all the way down.
- **Every trade in its own words.** A filling station fills its tanks, a diner
  orders ingredients, a dentist orders supplies, a software company takes on
  server capacity. Nobody stocks a shelf unless they actually have one. Your
  customers are patients, members, students, subscribers or diners, depending
  on what you do.
- **Stock, a supplier and a price.** Hold what you sell, buy it from a
  supplier you can haggle with or replace, and set what you charge — cheap
  wins custom and costs margin, dear does the reverse. Run out and you lose
  the sale; the trade goes to somebody else.
- **Eleven more things to do with it** — advertise, trade evenings and
  Sundays, insure the place, chase what customers owe you, smarten it up.

## People work for you

- **Your staff are real townspeople** with names, wages and lives. You choose
  who to take on and who to let go — the town does not staff your business for
  you.
- A closure puts named people out of work.

## Grow it, float it, or get out

- **An expansion ladder**: a second location, buying out your supplier,
  licensing your name to somebody else.
- **A ceiling you raise** rather than a wall you hit. A well-run business goes
  far further than one left alone.
- **Backers.** Sell a slice of your business — a seed round comes from a real
  person in town whose wallet actually pays and whose children inherit the
  stake; later rounds come from firms, who take a board seat and are correctly
  faceless.
- **Ten million inside eight years opens the exchange.** Your company lists on
  the same market as everyone else, with a real ticker, real shares and a real
  price.
- **Or sell the whole thing** — to a rival in your trade, to somebody in town,
  or to an outside firm when nobody local can afford it. Your backers are paid
  what they were promised before you see a penny. You can also simply shut it.

## Rivals, and taking a company over

- **The town has only so much custom.** Rival businesses in your trade divide
  it with you, and you can buy one out.
- **Buy your way into a listed company.** The stock screen now tells you what
  share of it you hold. A quarter blocks what the board wants; past half it is
  yours. The price runs away from you as you buy, so taking a whole company
  costs more than its market value — a decision, not arithmetic.
- **A seat comes with questions.** Past a blocking stake, a company puts things
  to its shareholders once a year: a buyback, a dividend cut to build a plant,
  a bid for the whole business, a chief executive's package. Past half, the
  vote is simply yours.
- **And somebody can come for you.** The richest person in town looks at what
  you floated, and if they can afford a real slice of it, they take one.

## Things that happen to you

Six of them, arriving on their own, roughly every other year:

> A rival opens up cheaper. Your best hand is offered more elsewhere. Your
> supplier goes under. A contract lands too big to be comfortable. The thing
> the whole place runs on fails. Somebody has their hand in the till.

None has an obviously right answer, every answer costs something, and standing
still is always allowed.

## You decide what the business keeps

- **The draw dial means what it says.** Choose 70/30 and you take 70% of the
  profit and 30% stays in as capital, exactly, every month. It used to be
  quietly overridden once the business filled up.
- **A business has a natural size.** You can leave in whatever share you like,
  but a business only earns on what it can actually put to work — a shop with
  fifty million in the till does not trade like a fifty-million business.
- **Once your business is worth half a million, it is the whole week.** You are
  asked outright: give it your full attention and leave the job, or keep the
  wage and run the business in the evenings. Keeping both is allowed, and it
  costs you — a business run in the evenings earns like one.

## Property

- **Rent out what you own.** Manage a portfolio: live in one, let another, find
  tenants, take the rent.
- **Property details** now show what a house has done for you — what you paid,
  what it is worth, the difference, and what it clears each month after upkeep
  when somebody is renting it.
- **Neighbourhoods drift** over the decades, with a market state you can read.
- **A strong credit file is asked for less down** — 10% rather than a flat
  fifth — and foreclosure is real.

## Money you can actually read

- **The money screen is yours alone.** No more of your spouse's wages or your
  father-in-law's pension counted as your income.
- **What you are worth, itemised** — every asset and every debt, adding to the
  exact figure at the top.
- **Every door you own counts.** A landlord's portfolio used to be worth
  nothing on paper; only the house you slept in was counted.
- **What your business pays you is income**, on the money screen and on your
  tax return, where it used to be neither.
- **The business books are a financial year**, accumulating from January and
  starting again, with the year just gone kept beside it.

## Fixes

- **Two money exploits.** Paying off a mortgage took nothing from your actual
  balance, and the casino minted free chips once your wallet ran dry. Both were
  the same disease: money was being written to a ledger no screen showed.
- Married couples: net worth reported a spouse as penniless while counting
  their debts in full.
- Bankruptcy could conjure positive money on discharge.
- Business books showed no expenses and a hundred per cent margin — they were
  counting the goods twice, once as stock you held and again as profit.
- Stocking up could tip a healthy business into losing months, because money
  on the shelf was treated as money gone rather than money in another place.
- Buying shares showed an instant loss the moment you owned them.
- The bank and the header disagreed about how much money you had.
- A business could shut with no warning at all. You are told now — and only
  when a year has gone wrong or the till is genuinely empty, not every time a
  single month dips.
- Deeds could be stranded on the dead: a landlord's houses were never
  inherited, never let and never sold again.
- Property details opened blank for anything you owned.
- Somebody could hold two elected offices at once.
- The town's businesses were nearly all software companies; they vary properly
  now.
- **Taking a military school slot said you had completed the course.** It
  announced a graduation on the spot, put nothing on your record, and left the
  same school on offer to be "completed" again and again. Taking a seat now
  sends you to the school: the class starts, the course runs, it can wash you
  out, and graduating is what earns the badge.
- Every button in the business screens reports what it did.

### A note for existing saves

Your save will open and upgrade itself. Two things will look different:

**Businesses earn differently.** The books were double-counting stock, and
fixing it changed what a month is worth. An established business may report
smaller profits than it did before — that money was never really there.

**Property bought before this update shows "not recorded" for what you paid.**
The deed never stored a purchase price. Anything bought from now on will show
what it cost and what it has gained.
